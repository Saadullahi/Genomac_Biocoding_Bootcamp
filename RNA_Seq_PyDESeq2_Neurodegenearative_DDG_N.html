What is RNA-Seq?¶

RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) is a high-throughput method used to measure gene expression by sequencing cDNA derived from RNA molecules.

The general workflow is:

  1. RNA extraction
  2. Library preparation
  3. Sequencing
  4. Read alignment (or pseudo-alignment)
  5. Counting reads per gene
  6. Statistical modeling of counts
  7. Identification of differentially expressed genes

In this notebook, we begin at Step 6 — using a gene count matrix.

Number of duplicate rows in Counts DataFrame: 36,061¶

This means that out of the total 62,754 genes (rows) in the counts DataFrame, 36,061 rows have identical expression values across all samples. In RNA-seq data, this often corresponds to genes that are either not expressed at all (all counts are zero) or genes that have very similar expression patterns across all samples, leading to identical count vectors.

While these are technically 'duplicates' in terms of their count profiles, they are usually distinct genes identified by their Geneid. It's important to remember that duplicated() checks for identical values across columns, not identical Geneid identifiers. If these are truly separate genes with the same expression pattern, it's not necessarily an error, but it does mean a significant portion of the data might be uninformative for differential expression (e.g., all zeros).

Interpretation of Descriptive Statistics for Counts DataFrame¶

The descriptive statistics provide a summary of the count distribution for each of the six samples (columns: Control_1, AD_1, AD_2, AD_3, Control_2, Control_3). Each column represents a sample, and the rows provide various statistical measures:

  • count: For all samples, the count is 62,754, which is the total number of genes in the dataset. This confirms there are no missing (NaN) values in any of the count columns.

  • mean: This is the average expression count for genes in each sample. We can see that the mean counts vary slightly across samples (e.g., Control_1 has a mean of ~11.89, while AD_1 has ~12.28). This difference is normal and will be addressed by the size factor estimation step in DESeq2 to normalize for sequencing depth differences.

  • std (Standard Deviation): This measures the spread or variability of gene counts within each sample. A high standard deviation indicates a wide range of expression values. The values are relatively large, suggesting considerable variability in gene expression across genes within each sample.

  • min: The minimum count for all samples is 0. This is expected in RNA-seq data, as many genes might not be expressed or have very low counts.

  • 25% (Q1): The first quartile. This indicates that at least 25% of the genes in each sample have a count of 0, further emphasizing the presence of many lowly expressed or unexpressed genes.

  • 50% (Median, Q2): The median count for all samples is 0. This means that at least half of the genes in each sample have zero counts. This is a very important observation, as it highlights the sparsity of the data (many genes are not detected or have very low expression).

  • 75% (Q3): The third quartile. This shows that 75% of genes have counts below this value. For example, in Control_1, 75% of genes have a count of 12 or less.

  • max: The maximum count represents the highest expression level observed for a single gene in each sample. These values are quite high (e.g., Control_1 has a max count of 81,146), indicating that a few genes are very highly expressed.

Overall:

The descriptive statistics confirm that the dataset contains typical RNA-seq count data characteristics: a large number of genes, many of which have zero counts, and a skewed distribution with a few highly expressed genes. The median count of 0 for all samples is particularly noteworthy and implies that a significant portion of the genome is not highly active in these specific conditions, or simply not detected by the sequencing depth. This sparsity needs to be handled appropriately by differential expression analysis tools like DESeq2, which accounts for such distributions.

The DESeq2 Statistical Model¶

DESeq2 models gene counts using a Negative Binomial distribution:

Counts ~ Negative Binomial(mean, dispersion)

Why not a Poisson model?

Poisson assumes: Variance = Mean

RNA-seq data shows: Variance > Mean (Overdispersion)

The Negative Binomial model introduces a dispersion parameter to account for biological variability.

What Happens During dds.deseq2()?¶

This performs three major steps:

  1. Size Factor Estimation

    • Normalizes for sequencing depth differences
    • Uses median-of-ratios method

  2. Dispersion Estimation

    • Estimates biological variability per gene
    • Shrinks dispersions toward a fitted trend

  3. Model Fitting

    • Fits a Negative Binomial GLM for each gene

Wald Test¶

After fitting the model, we test:

H0: log2FoldChange = 0 H1: log2FoldChange ≠ 0

The Wald statistic is:

β / SE(β)

Where:

  • β = estimated log2 fold change
  • SE = standard error of β

This gives a p-value per gene.

Summary of the Results¶

The above results DataFrame is the most important output of the differential expression analysis. It contains the statistical results for every gene tested with the following key summaries.

baseMean: This is the mean of the normalized count values, taken over all samples, for each gene. It's an indicator of the gene's average expression level. Genes with very low baseMean are often less reliable for differential expression analysis.

log2FoldChange: This is the estimated log2 fold change of gene expression between the two conditions compared (AD vs. control). A positive value means the gene is upregulated in AD, while a negative value means it's downregulated. For example, a log2FoldChange of 1 means a 2-fold increase, and -1 means a 2-fold decrease.

lfcSE: This is the standard error of the log2FoldChange estimate. A smaller standard error indicates a more precise estimate of the fold change.

stat: This is the Wald statistic, calculated as log2FoldChange / lfcSE. It's a measure of how far the observed log2FoldChange is from zero, relative to its standard error.

pvalue: This is the nominal p-value from the Wald test, indicating the probability of observing a log2FoldChange as extreme as, or more extreme than, the one observed, assuming the null hypothesis (no differential expression) is true.

padj: This is the adjusted p-value, which is the p-value corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. This is the most crucial metric for determining statistical significance, as it controls the False Discovery Rate (FDR). Typically, genes with padj < 0.05 are considered statistically significant

Multiple Testing Problem¶

In RNA-seq, we test thousands of genes simultaneously.

If we use p < 0.05: We expect 5% false positives by chance.

Solution: Benjamini-Hochberg correction

This controls the False Discovery Rate (FDR).

Adjusted p-values are called: padj

Why Log2 Fold Change Shrinkage?¶

Low-count genes often show exaggerated fold changes.

Shrinkage:

  • Pulls extreme fold changes toward zero
  • Improves stability
  • Makes effect sizes more reliable
  • Improves ranking of genes

This is strongly recommended for interpretation.

Interpretation¶

The descriptive statistics for the baseMean of these NaN padj genes are crucial. They show that all 18,236 genes with NaN adjusted p-values have a baseMean of 0.0. This is the most common reason for NaN values in DESeq2 results.

This happens because:

DESeq2 (and pydeseq2) performs internal filtering to remove genes that have very low or zero counts across all samples.

For these genes:

  1. No reliable estimation: The model cannot reliably estimate parameters like log2FoldChange or dispersion for genes with zero or extremely low expression, as there isn't enough data to perform a meaningful statistical test.
  1. Increased power: Removing these genes helps to increase the statistical power for the remaining genes by reducing the number of multiple comparisons.

In essence, these 18,236 genes were likely filtered out by pydeseq2 because they had no counts across any of the samples, making differential expression analysis for them impossible or unreliable. This is expected behavior in RNA-seq analysis.

Interpretation of points and regions¶

The Volcano Plot is a powerful visualization that helps us understand the relationship between the magnitude of gene expression changes (effect size) and their statistical significance.

X-axis (Log2 Fold Change): This represents the change in gene expression between the AD and control conditions. A positive value means the gene is upregulated in AD, while a negative value means it's downregulated. The further a point is from zero on the x-axis, the greater the magnitude of the change.

Y-axis (-log10 Adjusted p-value (FDR)): This axis shows the statistical significance of the differential expression. Higher values on the y-axis correspond to smaller (more significant) adjusted p-values. A common threshold for significance is padj < 0.05, which would appear as a horizontal line at -log10(0.05) ≈ 1.3.

Red points: These genes are highlighted because their absolute log2FoldChange is greater than 2 (|log2FoldChange| > 2). This means their expression level changed by at least 4-fold (either up or down) between AD and Control conditions. These genes are considered to have a biologically significant effect size.

Gray points: These are genes with an absolute log2FoldChange less than or equal to 2, indicating smaller changes in expression.

Green dashed horizontal line: This line represents the statistical significance threshold, where padj = 0.05. Genes plotted above this line are considered statistically significant.

Purple dashed vertical lines: These lines are at Log2FC = -2 and Log2FC = 2. They mark the boundaries for genes with at least a 4-fold change in expression.

Interpreting the Regions:

Upper-right region (above green, to the right of purple +2 line): Genes here are both significantly upregulated and show a large fold change (at least 4-fold increase). These are typically the most interesting genes for further study.

Upper-left region (above green, to the left of purple -2 line): Genes here are both significantly downregulated and show a large fold change (at least 4-fold decrease). These are also highly interesting candidates.

Genes between the purple vertical lines and above the green horizontal line: These genes are statistically significant but have a smaller fold change (less than 4-fold).

Genes below the green horizontal line: These genes are not considered statistically significant, regardless of their fold change.

Looking at the plot, we can visually identify genes that are strong candidates for differential expression based on both their magnitude of change and statistical confidence.

Conclusion¶

The PyDESeq2 analysis of gene expression profiles between Alzheimer's Disease (AD) and healthy control samples, based on the provided gene count matrix, did not identify any statistically significant differentially expressed genes, even when relaxed thresholds (padj < 0.1 and |log2FoldChange| > 0.5) were used. This outcome is primarily driven by the following key factors:

Small Sample Size: With only 3 biological replicates per condition, the statistical power to detect subtle yet significant gene expression changes is severely limited. DESeq2's statistical model relies on sufficient replicates to accurately estimate biological dispersion (variability), which is crucial for robust p-value calculation.

Dispersion Estimation Challenges: The dds.deseq2() run issued a warning: "The dispersion trend curve fitting did not converge." This indicates an issue in modeling the gene-specific variability, likely exacerbated by the small sample size. Consequently, we were unable to visualize the dispersion plot, and the accuracy of the adjusted p-values might be compromised.

High Intrinsic Variability: The raw count data displayed high gene-wise variability (large standard deviations and CVs), which is typical for RNA-seq. However, with limited replicates, distinguishing true biological signal from noise becomes difficult.

No Clear Strong Signals: Despite some genes showing large |log2FoldChange| values in the MA plot, these changes did not achieve statistical significance after multiple testing correction, suggesting that these observed fold changes might be due to chance or high variability rather than consistent biological differences.

In conclusion, while the current analysis didn't identify significant individual genes, the methodology was sound given the data limitations. The path forward involves strengthening the experimental design, particularly by increasing the sample size, to enable a more powerful and conclusive differential expression analysis.

Actionable Insights & Recommendations¶

Given these findings, here are actionable insights and recommendations for moving forward:

1. Increase Sample Size: This is the most crucial recommendation. Obtaining more biological replicates for both the AD and control groups would drastically increase the statistical power of the analysis, allowing DESeq2 to more accurately estimate dispersion and detect potentially subtle, but true, differential expression. Aim for at least 6-10 replicates per group if feasible.

2. Re-evaluate Dispersion Estimation: Address the "dispersion trend curve fitting did not converge" warning. With a larger sample size, this issue might resolve itself. If not, consider alternative fit_type parameters in dds.deseq2() (e.g., fit_type="local" or fit_type="mean") if the default "parametric" continues to fail, although a parametric fit is generally preferred.

3. Consider Pathway/Gene Set Enrichment Analysis: Since individual gene-level significance was not achieved, consider if groups of genes (e.g., genes within a specific pathway or known AD-related gene sets) show subtle but coordinated changes. Tools like GSEA or GO/KEGG enrichment could reveal pathway-level deregulation even if individual genes aren't strongly significant.

4. Alternative Differential Expression Methods: While DESeq2 is highly regarded, exploring other robust differential expression tools like edgeR or limma-voom could provide alternative perspectives, especially if issues with DESeq2's dispersion estimation persist. Some tools may perform better with very small sample sizes or specific data characteristics.

5. Validation of High Fold Change Genes: For genes with a high |log2FoldChange| but not statistical significance, consider validating their expression changes using orthogonal methods (e.g., qRT-PCR) if biological resources allow. This could help determine if these are false positives or true biological signals missed due to limited statistical power.

6. Review Experimental Design/Data Quality: Confirm that sample preparation, sequencing depth, and read alignment were optimal and consistent across all samples. High technical variability can also mask biological signals.
